Unreplaced

Skills AI can't replace.

AI isn't so much eliminating jobs en masse as changing what work consists of, and people at the start of their careers feel it most. This program doesn't teach tools, which change quickly. It focuses on what stays: how you think, how you decide, how you read a situation, and how you work with people.

Who it's for and why

Seven skills the reports identify as key.

For people who feel uneasy about AI-driven change and would rather deal with it than pretend it isn't happening.

  • Critical thinking
  • Creativity
  • Adaptability
  • Mental resilience
  • Communication and empathy
  • Initiative and ownership
  • Learning

What we don't teach

Prompting, how to operate AI tools, data analysis or cybersecurity. That's a deliberate choice: these skills are already widely available, and knowledge of any specific tool goes out of date within months.

AI appears in the program as a subject for reflection and as material for practising critical judgment. You work on situations from your own experience, in a stable group.

"People often ask me: will AI take my job? The real question is: what do I bring that can't just be generated by AI? Most of us never had to ask ourselves that question, because doing our job well used to be enough. That era is ending."

Aga Skraburska, author and facilitator of the program

Founder and Board President of the Foundation. Graduate of the Institute of Sociology at the Jagiellonian University. A trainer and coach for twenty years, Head of Product Education at Nexthink.

Session plan

Eight sessions, one cycle.

01

What's actually changing

We start by getting the facts straight: what AI does well today, what it doesn't, and where its responsibility ends. Then we turn to your own situation: you look at which of your tasks AI could take over, and what depends on you: your judgment, your experience and your relationships with people. For many people, this is the first moment unease turns into something concrete.

After this session: you can tell the scale of the direct threat apart from the narrowing of entry-level roles.

02

Critical thinking

When answers come instantly and sound confident, the biggest risk isn't the machine getting it wrong — it's that we stop checking. In this session we practise asking better questions, spotting our own assumptions, and making decisions when the information is incomplete, which is the rule, not the exception.

After this session: you can assess how reliable an AI output is and know what to check before making a decision.

03

Creativity

Creativity isn't artistic talent. It's the ability to connect things that don't seem to belong together, and to notice a problem before anyone's put it into words. AI models are great at generating variations on what already exists. They're much weaker at asking whether we're even working on the right problem. That's what we practise here.

After this session: you can propose non-obvious solutions and spot a problem before anyone has put it into words.

04

Adaptability

Change rarely hurts on its own. What hurts is losing predictability and the sense of being good at something — after years of competence, you're a beginner again. This session is about getting through that period without faking enthusiasm or gritting your teeth. About learning faster, and making peace with a stretch of genuine discomfort.

After this session: you have a method for working through situations with no ready-made procedure.

05

Mental resilience

It's hard to think clearly with constant background worry about the future. We talk about what actually builds stability: what's within your control and what isn't, how to tell worry apart from information, and where to draw your sense of worth from when work stops providing it. No forced reassurance.

After this session: you can tell worry apart from information and act under uncertainty.

06

Communication and empathy

The more gets automated, the more depends on conversation — on whether you can persuade someone, listen carefully, or say a hard thing in a way the other person can actually hear. It's the oldest skill we have, and right now it's also the most underrated. We practise it on situations from your own work.

After this session: you're ready for difficult conversations, careful listening and working in a group.

07

Initiative and ownership

This isn't about starting a company. It's about a mindset: noticing a problem and dealing with it before anyone tells you to. In a world where simply following instructions loses value the fastest, that's one of the few things that genuinely strengthens your position. We work on what's been holding you back from it.

After this session: you take initiative without being asked and deal with the problems you notice.

08

Your plan and your group

At the end, you put it all into a simple plan: what you're learning, what you're changing about how you work, and how you'll know it's heading the right way. We're also honest that resolutions like this usually fade within two weeks if no one else knows about them — which is why we finish as a group, not alone.

After this session: you have a personal development plan and a method for learning new skills on your own.

These workshops are educational and developmental in nature. We do not provide professional or legal advice.

Our research

A program that is itself being studied.

Alongside the workshops, we also conduct research: we look at how young adults perceive AI's impact on their own professional future, and whether taking part in Unreplaced changes that perception. We study this in every edition.

Taking part in the research is voluntary and independent of taking part in the sessions. Data is collected in anonymised form, based on informed consent. In the editions with the Jagiellonian University, the Institute of Sociology provides academic supervision and the results belong to the Institute: the Foundation has no influence over their content or conclusions.

The questions we start from

  1. How do young adults define the threat AI poses to their own career path?
  2. Which skills do they consider hard to replace and which at risk, and how far do those views match market data?
  3. How can we explain very high adoption of AI tools alongside a very low sense of readiness to work with them?
  4. Does taking part in the program change the level of perceived threat and the sense of agency?
